UNDERSTANDING PHYSIOTHERAPY

More than treatment.
A plan for movement.

Physiotherapy supports movement and function affected by injury, illness or disability. An assessment helps identify your needs; care may include exercise, hands-on techniques and advice for daily activities.

It can be part of care for joint and muscle problems, recovery after surgery, or movement difficulties following a stroke. Your therapist explains the options and adapts the plan to you.

Source: NHS — Physiotherapy ↗
CONDITIONS & CARE

Understand what you're feeling.

These guides explain common concerns; symptoms alone cannot confirm a diagnosis. Physiotherapy can support recovery and symptom management, but does not promise a cure. Your treatment depends on an individual assessment.

01 / BACK & SPINE

Lower back pain & sciatica

Back pain may relate to a strain or other conditions, including a slipped disc or sciatica. The cause is not always obvious. An assessment can help decide the right next step.

How care may help

Staying active within your comfort and suitable exercise can help. Physiotherapy may include guided movement and hands-on care. Avoid prolonged bed rest; seek advice if pain worsens or limits everyday activities.

When to get urgent help

Back pain with new bladder or bowel changes, numbness around the genitals, or symptoms in both legs needs emergency assessment. Severe pain after a serious accident also needs urgent care.

Source: NHS — Back pain ↗

02 / JOINT HEALTH

Knee pain & osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis can cause joint pain and stiffness. It has no cure, but treatment can help manage symptoms and make daily activities easier. Other causes of knee pain need their own assessment.

How care may help

Regular exercise is an important part of osteoarthritis care. A tailored programme can work on muscle strength and general fitness. Physiotherapy can help you learn suitable exercises and ways to move more comfortably.

A plan for your everyday life

Discuss activities that are difficult, such as stairs, walking or getting up from a chair. Your clinician can adjust your plan and discuss other treatment options if symptoms continue.

Source: NHS — Osteoarthritis treatment ↗

Explore knee & joint care →

03 / SHOULDER MOVEMENT

Frozen shoulder

A frozen shoulder is painful and stiff, and can make arm movement difficult. Recovery can take months or longer; there is no single timeline for everyone.

How care may help

A physiotherapist assesses your movement and may guide gentle stretching and strengthening. Avoid forcing painful movements. Your doctor can discuss pain management when needed.

When to seek assessment

Arrange a review when pain or stiffness persists, or when it becomes difficult to move your arm. Your plan should match your current movement and symptoms.

Source: NHS — Frozen shoulder ↗

Explore shoulder care →

04 / NEUROLOGICAL REHABILITATION

Recovery after a stroke

A stroke affects people differently. Recovery may take weeks, months or years, and rehabilitation needs to reflect each person's abilities and priorities.

How care may help

Physiotherapy can support movement as part of a wider rehabilitation plan. Other professionals may be needed for speech, swallowing, thinking or emotional wellbeing. These are distinct areas of care.

Involving family

A home recovery plan, shared goals and caregiver involvement can support progress. Follow the hospital team's instructions and discuss changes with them. New, sudden stroke symptoms require emergency care, not a routine physiotherapy booking.

Source: NHS — Stroke recovery ↗

GENTLE MOVEMENT LIBRARY

A gentle place to begin.

Before you start: These are general examples, not your personal exercise plan. Ask your clinician about suitability, especially after surgery, stroke or an injury, or if you have balance difficulties. Use a stable chair without wheels. Stop if an exercise causes pain, dizziness or new symptoms.
01

Seated marching

  1. Sit upright and hold the chair's sides.
  2. Lift one bent knee within a comfortable range.
  3. Lower your foot slowly, then change sides.

Start gently; ask your therapist to set the number of repetitions.

02

Ankle movement

  1. Sit upright, supported by a stable chair.
  2. Lift and straighten one leg comfortably.
  3. Slowly point the toes away, then bring them back towards you.
  4. Lower the leg and repeat on the other side.
03

Gentle neck turns

  1. Sit upright with relaxed shoulders.
  2. Slowly turn your head to one side, only as far as comfortable.
  3. Return to the centre, then turn to the other side.

Keep the movement smooth. Never force a stretch.

Adapted from NHS sitting exercises ↗. Follow the linked guidance and your own clinician's advice.

FEEL READY FOR YOUR VISIT

A little preparation.
A comfortable start.

Make room for a conversation, assessment and a plan you understand.

What should I have ready?+

Keep relevant medical reports, surgical instructions and a list of medicines available. Wear comfortable clothing and prepare a clear space for assessment.

What should I tell my physiotherapist?+

Describe your concerns, what makes them better or worse, and what you want to do more comfortably. Mention recent surgery, injuries and any restrictions your doctor has given you.

Can a family member be present?+

If you want a family member or caregiver to support you, let us know when arranging your visit. We can discuss how they may help with your care plan.

Will I receive exercises?+

If suitable, your physiotherapist will explain exercises selected for your assessment. Ask how often to do them, how they should feel and when to stop or seek advice.
